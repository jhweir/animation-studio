# Learning to evolve

> First draft (941 words, about 6:00). The philosophical companion to the broken app
> paradigm. Spine: social technology already evolves, but only at the crudest level, by whole
> products living and dying. Life's history is evolution learning to evolve better, moving trial
> and error inward. Then a second turn: forests and bodies, what integration takes, and where it
> must stop. Ends on collective intelligence as an open question, not a claim.
>
> Sources behind the ideas (not named on screen): Campbell on vicarious selection, Dennett's tower
> of generate-and-test, Kirschner and Gerhart on facilitated variation, Maynard Smith and
> Szathmáry on major transitions, Michod and Godfrey-Smith on transitions in individuality.

## 1. It already evolves {#already-evolves}

Every social app you've ever used was an experiment.

Someone had an idea about how people should talk, share or decide, and built it. Millions tried it.
Most of those experiments died. A few survived, and the next ones copied what worked.

So social technology already evolves. But it evolves in the slowest, most wasteful way there is.

## 2. Evolution by death {#by-death}

Look at how it works. What gets tested is a whole app, all of it at once. The variation comes from a
handful of teams. And the test is brutal: does the company survive?

A brilliant idea inside a failing app dies with it. A bad idea inside a winning app lives on,
because the whole thing gets kept. And what survives is whatever keeps the company alive, which isn't
always what's good for the people inside.

That's evolution at its crudest: whole things, tested by death.

## 3. Life learned to evolve {#life-learned}

Life started out like that too. But the remarkable thing about its history isn't only that life
evolved. It's that evolution itself kept getting better.

Recombination let good ideas from separate lineages come together, instead of competing until one
died. Some bacteria learned to pass useful genes sideways, so an innovation could jump to strangers
without waiting generations.

Immune systems brought evolution inside the body: they vary, test and multiply defences within days,
not millennia. Brains went further. An animal could try things in its own lifetime and keep what
worked. Then imagine them first, so that ideas could die instead of us. And with culture, we began
inheriting what others had learned.

Each step moved trial and error inward. The units got smaller. The cycles got faster. And failure
got cheaper, until it no longer cost the whole.

## 4. The bottom rung {#bottom-rung}

On that ladder, our social technology is still on the bottom rung.

The tools that shape how billions of people understand the world, and act together, still improve
only when whole products are born and die. We use the most advanced technology we've ever built,
and it evolves like the earliest life.

## 5. Moving evolution inside {#inside}

So what would it look like to climb?

Make the unit smaller. Not whole apps, but parts: a way of deciding, a way of sharing, a way of
showing what matters first. Let anyone build a part. Let communities choose the parts they need by
living with them. And let parts be built from other parts, and travel between communities, the way
genes jump between bacteria.

Then trial and error moves inside a community's own life. A group can try a new way of making
decisions this month, and keep it if it works. A failed experiment costs one part, not a company.
And a good idea doesn't die with its app. It spreads.

## 6. Forest and body {#forest-and-body}

But a heap of parts isn't enough. Nature shows two very different ways that living things come
together.

A forest is an ecosystem. Thousands of species, each living for itself. It's diverse, resilient,
endlessly inventive. But no one in it shares a purpose.

A body is different. Its cells have joined their fates together. They specialise, coordinate, and
act as one, and only succeed if the whole does.

Our social tools manage to fail both ways. A sealed app is like a plantation: orderly and
integrated, but designed once, owned, and harvested. And a group spread across a dozen tools is like
scattered scrub: lots of pieces, none of them working together.

What we need is both, at different levels. A forest of parts, where anyone can build and new ideas
keep appearing. And in each community, those parts coming together as one body, serving that group's
purpose.

## 7. What it takes to become a body {#integration}

Life has made that leap, from loose collections to integrated beings, many times. And each time it
needed the same few things.

The parts' fates have to be tied to the whole. A part that thrives by spreading, whether or not the
body does well, is what biology calls cancer. In social software, it's the feature built to go viral
at the community's expense. So it matters, more than anything, who decides what spreads. If it's the
communities living with the parts, success means helping communities thrive.

The body needs defences too: ways to see what a part does, and stop the ones that cause harm. And it
needs shared conventions, so that parts made by strangers fit together at all.

## 8. Where it must stop {#limit}

There's a limit, though, and it matters.

Cells give up everything to the body. They can't leave, and they can't belong to anything else.
People must never have to. Taken all the way, integration is the hive.

So the goal isn't to turn people into cells. It's for each group to set its own balance between
forest and body, and for every person to keep the freedom to belong to many groups, and to walk away
from any of them.

## 9. What it's for {#for}

Why does this matter so much?

Because these aren't just apps. They're the tools we think together with. They shape what we notice,
what we understand, and what we can achieve together. If they can evolve, the way we think together
can evolve too.

Each great transition in the history of life produced something new: beings that could do what none
of their parts could do alone. Maybe the next one isn't biological. Maybe it's groups of people
learning to sense, decide and adapt together, far better than we do now, while staying free.

## 10. Learning to evolve {#close}

Life didn't just evolve. It learned how to evolve better.

Our social technology hasn't learned that yet. It still waits for whole things to die.

Whether it learns is up to us, and to how we choose to build.
