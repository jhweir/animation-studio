# Where is the understanding?

> Second draft, for review before any animation work. About 970 words of narration, roughly 6:15
> at the draft voice's pace. The angle: the Chinese Room is the intuition behind most "it's just predicting
> tokens" dismissals of language models, and the Systems Reply is the strongest answer to it.
> Blockquotes under each scene are the visual plan; the voice ignores them.
>
> Visual through-line: one camera that keeps pulling back. The film's argument is that the room
> trains you to look at the wrong scale, so every scene answers a "zoom in" with a "zoom out". The
> room is drawn small and warm at first (a lamp, a desk, a slot in the door) and becomes cold,
> vast and diagrammatic as the real size of the rulebook arrives.
>
> Style: the Coasys style is built for WE films. This probably wants its own, quieter style
> (paper, ink, one accent colour for "meaning"). To decide before scene work starts.

## 1. The question {#question}

Ask a language model a hard question, and it answers fluently. It explains a joke. It argues back.
It catches its own mistake.

So does it understand anything? Or is it just shuffling symbols it has never understood?

The most famous answer to that question is older than the internet. And it says no.

> A chat exchange types itself out in the centre of a dark frame: a question, a fluent answer.
> On "shuffling symbols" the answer's words come loose and drift as tiles. On "older than the
> internet" the tiles settle into the shape of a closed door, with a slot in it. Title card
> over the door: "Where is the understanding?"

## 2. The room {#room}

In 1980, the philosopher John Searle asked you to imagine a room.

Inside sits a man who speaks only English. Slips of paper covered in Chinese characters come in
through a slot in the door. He has no idea what they mean.

But he has a rulebook. It tells him, in English, which shapes to write back for whichever shapes
come in. He follows it, step by step, and passes his answer out through the slot.

Outside, a native speaker reads the answers and finds them perfect. As far as she can tell, there's
someone in that room who understands Chinese.

> The camera goes through the slot into the room: lamp, desk, man, one thick book. A card
> comes in; he looks it up, copies out shapes, posts the reply. Outside, the reader smiles and
> writes the next question. Keep the characters as abstract glyph shapes, not real Chinese,
> so viewers who read Chinese aren't sidetracked by the actual content.

## 3. Searle's conclusion {#conclusion}

But the man doesn't understand a word. He's only matching shapes.

And that, Searle said, is all a computer ever does. A program follows rules for symbols, without
knowing what any of them mean. So however well a machine seems to talk, there's nobody home.

It's a powerful picture. Many people find it settles the question. But it only works if you look
where the picture tells you to look.

> Close on the man's face: blank, patient. Thought bubble with a question mark. On "all a
> computer ever does" the room cross-fades into a chip: man → processor, book → memory, slot
> → input/output. On "where the picture tells you to look" a spotlight narrows onto the man
> alone, and the rest of the frame darkens.

## 4. The size of the book {#scale}

Start with the book. The picture shows one volume on a desk. But think about what it would really
have to contain: rules for any question, any joke, any follow-up, in any context, from anyone.

Today's language models are the nearest thing we've built to that book. A large one is a hundred
billion numbers or more. Printed out, it would fill tens of thousands of volumes. Worked through
by hand, at one step a second, a single word of its reply would take thousands of years.

The man isn't having a quiet afternoon at a desk. He's the smallest part of a vast machine, and
it's that machine that's answering.

> The book on the desk starts to multiply: shelves, then corridors, then a library seen from
> above, the room a speck inside it. A counter ticks up beside the shelves to the number of
> volumes. Then a clock face at the desk: the man's hand moving one step per tick, a calendar
> beside him flipping years, then centuries, while one word slowly forms on the output slip.
> End on a wide shot: the tiny figure inside the enormous system. This scene carries the
> film; it should feel like vertigo.

## 5. The systems reply {#systems}

Which is where the most famous reply to Searle comes in.

Of course the man doesn't understand Chinese. He's one component, like a single chip in a computer.
The question isn't whether he understands. It's whether the whole system does: the man, the
rulebook, the notes, the whole process running together.

Now follow a question through your own head. The sound reaches your ear and passes through a relay
deep in your brain, which sends it up into the neocortex, the folded outer layer of your brain.

That's where most of what you know is kept, in the strength of over a hundred trillion connections
between cells. It's your rulebook. But unlike the book in the room, it also does the work. The same
connections that store what you know handle the sound, then the meaning, then the choice of words,
then the movement, until the answer comes out through your lips and tongue.

The room splits those two jobs apart: a book to hold the rules, and a man to carry them out. Brains
don't work that way. Neither do language models, where the same numbers both hold what the model
knows and do the computing.

Stop at any point along the way and ask whether this part understands English. None of them do.
Each cell just passes signals on, the way its inputs tell it to. And nobody takes that as proof that
you don't understand English.

Your understanding isn't in any one of those parts. It's held in how they're connected: in the
pattern of the whole.

> The question arrives at an ear as a sound wave and travels as a pulse of light through a
> side-on brain. Its first stop is the thalamus, drawn to echo the room: signals coming in
> through it like cards through the slot. This is the man's place in the brain, and it
> understands nothing either.
>
> On "neocortex" the pulse fans out from the thalamus across the folded outer sheet, which
> lights faintly as a dense mesh of connections. A counter (over 100 trillion) sits beside it,
> deliberately echoing scene 4's model counter (100 billion), so the scales can be compared.
> On "your rulebook", a ghost of the room's book is laid over the sheet, then fades as the
> sheet starts working.
>
> The pulse then moves through stations within the neocortex: auditory cortex, Wernicke's
> area, Broca's area, motor cortex, then out through the brainstem to the mouth. Small labels
> name each stop on screen, while the narration stays plain.
>
> "Splits those two jobs apart": a three-panel beat. The room pulls apart into a book labelled
> "store" and a man labelled "work", with a gap between them. In the brain and in the model, the
> same mesh carries both labels at once, with no gap.
>
> "Stop at any point": a quick zoom in at each station shows plain tissue and firing cells with
> a "?" that stays unlit, then pulls back.
>
> On "the pattern of the whole", the camera pulls back to the whole brain and the accent colour
> (meaning) appears for the first time. It doesn't sit on any one station; it spreads across the
> connections of the whole circuit. A matching inset shows the room-library system lighting up
> the same way.
>
> Keep the route simplified and honest: it's a sketch of the main path, not the whole story.
> No arrow should suggest a central "control room" where the real thinking happens.

## 6. Searle's comeback {#comeback}

Searle had an answer. Let the man memorise the entire rulebook, and do everything in his head.
Now he is the whole system. And he still doesn't understand Chinese.

But look at what that does. There are now two things going on in one head. There's the man, who
speaks English and is bored. And there's the process he's running, which answers in fluent Chinese.

The man can't see what that process understands, any more than you can feel your neurons firing.
Him not understanding Chinese doesn't tell you whether the thing he's running does.

> The library pours into the man's head; the room dissolves and he stands in an open field,
> lips moving. Inside his head, two layers: his own thoughts in English (a grocery list, a
> yawn) and, beneath them, a dense running process of glyphs, lit in the accent colour. A
> one-way glass between the layers: he looks down and sees only shapes. This is the virtual
> mind reply; we don't name it, we show it.

## 7. What the room really shows {#shows}

So the Chinese Room doesn't prove that machines can't understand. What it shows is how strongly our
intuitions depend on where we're told to look.

Zoom in on any mind far enough and you'll find something blind doing what it's told. That's true of
a man with a book, of a chip, and of a neuron. If that ruled out understanding, it would rule out
yours too.

There's a second trick in the room. The man feels no understanding, so we conclude there's none
there. But understanding and experience aren't the same thing. You still understand English while
you're asleep. Whether understanding is felt from the inside is a separate question, and the room
only works by blurring the two.

Some say today's models are a frozen rulebook, while ours keeps changing as we learn. That's true,
for now. A model learns from what's in front of it in a conversation, but nothing it learns there
changes the model itself. People who can no longer form new memories are in the same position, and
they still understand what you say to them. A frozen rulebook limits what a model can learn. It
doesn't stop it understanding. And models that keep learning from experience are already being
worked on.

None of this proves that today's models do understand. That's still open. But it means the question
can't be settled by pointing at the parts. You have to ask what the whole system can actually do.

> Recap montage at three scales, left to right: man and book, chip, neuron. Each one zooms in
> to a blind mechanism, then pulls back to the whole lit in the accent colour. On "rule out
> yours too" the third panel's whole becomes a person, the viewer's stand-in.
>
> "A second trick": the person from the recap lies asleep. Their neocortex mesh stays lit in
> the accent colour (the understanding is still there) while the thought bubble above them is
> empty (nothing is being experienced). Beside them, the man in the room: bubble full of
> boredom, the system around him lit. Two separate dials, "understanding" and "experience", for
> each figure. The point is that they can come apart.
>
> "A frozen rulebook": the model's mesh drawn with its connections locked (pinned, greyed
> strokes), and a small notepad beside it (the conversation) that fills and is then wiped at
> the end of the chat. In the brain panel, a matching notepad (the hippocampus) whose contents
> are carried into the neocortex mesh overnight, the connections shifting. Then the amnesia
> beat: the brain panel's carry-over arrow is cut, the notepad still fills and wipes, and the
> person still answers a question with the accent colour lit. Don't name or depict a real
> patient. On "already being worked on", the model's pinned strokes start to loosen.
>
> On "still open"
> the accent colour on the language model's panel dims to a question mark rather than
> claiming the answer.

## 8. Close {#close}

Searle asked whether there's anyone in the room who understands Chinese.

The better question is whether there's anyone in the room at all. Or whether the room, the book and
the man, taken together, are what's doing the understanding.

> Back to the opening door with the slot, now seen from far outside: the whole structure,
> library and all, glowing faintly. A card slides out of the slot. Fade to the title:
> "Where is the understanding?"
