# The broken app paradigm

> Focused version (883 words, about 5:30). Built from 2026-11-10-full but narrowed to the app
> paradigm alone: a short stakes hook, then the user and builder problems, root cause, precedents,
> why social never opened up, why now, the console, and how an ecosystem gets around network effects.
> The wider social-dilemma material (misalignment, funding, collective intelligence) is left for a
> separate film. The tenants' association remains the concrete thread.

## 1. The wrong shape {#wrong-shape}

The tools communities use to talk, organise and decide shape what those communities can become. Yet
almost all of them are built the same way: one team, one sealed application, designed once and
handed to millions.

That shape is the problem. It's failing the people who use these tools and the people who build
them.

## 2. The user's side {#users}

Take a tenants' association: two hundred households in one block. They need to share news, raise
problems, make decisions and hold their landlord to account.

They use a group chat, where important things scroll away. A social media group, where a feed
decides what members see. And a spreadsheet nobody updates.

None of these tools were made for them. Each arrived with one shape, designed for everyone. The group
can't change what shows first, how decisions get made, or how the space works as it grows from
twenty active members to two hundred.

And there are hundreds of thousands of groups like this, each needing something different, each
changing over time. Even the most caring design team can only serve the common denominator. So
communities conform to whatever fits least badly, or scatter across several tools and lose what came
from being connected.

## 3. The builder's side {#builders}

Now the other side. Somewhere, someone has built exactly what that association needs: a fair way for
a group to raise issues and decide on them together.

To get it to anyone, they've had to wrap it in a whole application: database, servers, logins,
notifications, hosting. Months of foundations that thousands of teams have built before, just to
reach the part that's theirs. Then comes the hardest part: persuading a group to leave the tools they
know, move their people and their history, and bet on a new app that may not last.

So the association never finds it. And a voting tool, a map, a chat and a shared calendar, which
could work side by side over the same data and make each other more useful, get built as islands,
competing for the same attention.

## 4. One root cause {#root-cause}

These are one problem seen from two ends. Social software gets built as finished, sealed
applications, when what communities need are spaces they can assemble from parts, and that anyone
can build parts for.

## 5. Other fields already did this {#other-fields}

Other fields have made this move. Games opened up to mods, and some mods outgrew the games they
started in. Web publishing opened up to themes and plugins, and WordPress came to run around four in
ten websites.

The recipe was the same each time. Keep the core small and the conventions clear. Let anyone build
pieces that fit, and let people assemble what they need. What grows from that isn't a product. It's
an ecosystem.

## 6. Why social never did {#why-not-social}

Social platforms have tried versions of this, opening up to apps, bots and third-party clients. But
each opening happened on the platform's terms, and when it stopped suiting them, they shut it.

That's because control of the interface is where their business model lives. And opening a social
space is genuinely risky: code running inside it can read private messages and act in people's
names. So an open layer for social software has to solve the safety problem, and it can't belong to
any one company.

## 7. Why now {#why-now}

Something has shifted. AI is making software cheap to build. Soon a group will be able to describe
the tool it needs and get one.

On its own, that makes things worse. A million more sealed apps are a million more islands. When
building is cheap, the bottleneck becomes everything else: trust, reaching people, and getting pieces
to work together. That's exactly what a shared, open layer provides.

## 8. A console no one owns {#console}

WE works like a games console for social software. The console holds the trusted parts: the building
blocks, the engine that draws them, and the rules for what a cartridge is allowed to do. Each
experience is a cartridge: a forum, a voting tool, a whole community app. Swap the cartridge and the
same space becomes something new.

But unlike any real console, no one owns this one. It's open source, and anyone can make a
cartridge. You can open one and see exactly what it does before you let it in. You can build new
ones from pieces of old ones, and any community can pass them on. And it doesn't own your data: it
plugs into whatever a community already uses.

## 9. Building together, not alone {#together}

Now a builder doesn't have to win users from scratch. They make the one cartridge they care about,
and it can travel to every community that wants it, inside the spaces they already have. The
tenants' association adds the voting tool without moving anywhere.

In a sealed world, every new user makes one company's app more valuable. In an open ecosystem, every
new cartridge makes every space more capable, and every new community makes every cartridge more
worth building. Ideas that used to compete start adding up.

And what spreads is decided by the communities that use it. Many hands try things. What serves
people spreads, what doesn't fades, and the whole keeps adapting. Software stops being a finished
product handed down, and starts behaving like the communities that use it: a living system.

Social software has always been something they make for us. Now it can be something we make together.
