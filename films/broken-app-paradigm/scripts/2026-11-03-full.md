# The broken app paradigm

> The full version (866 words, about 5:25), as first sent to the bots.

## 1. Fighting on their ground {#their-ground}

A handful of companies now shape how billions of people talk, find news, organise and see each
other. It crept in slowly, and now it's everywhere. Most people feel there's no way to push back.
Alternatives appear, and fade.

Part of the reason is that every challenger keeps fighting on their ground. Almost every social tool
is built the same way: one company, one sealed application, handed to millions, finished. In that
game the biggest app wins, and they already have the biggest apps.

That's not just a problem with one bad app. The paradigm itself is broken, and it's failing the
people who use these tools and the people who build them.

## 2. The user's side {#users}

Start with the people using these tools.

There are hundreds of thousands of communities online, and each needs different things: what to see
first, how to decide, how to talk. Inside each one, every person's needs differ again.

And communities are alive. What works for ten people breaks at a thousand. What a group needs this
month isn't what it needs next.

But the tools don't change with them. They arrive with one shape, designed once for everyone. Even
the most caring design team can only serve the common denominator, and everyone outside it gets left
behind.

So communities either conform to whichever app fits least badly, or fragment across several and
lose what came from being connected.

## 3. The builder's side {#builders}

Now the other side. Say you have an idea for a better social tool: a new way to vote, a map of your
bioregion, a game that helps a group decide.

To get it to anyone, you have to wrap it in a whole application: database, servers, logins,
notifications, design system, hosting. That's months of foundations thousands of teams have built
before you, just to reach the part that's yours. AI speeds up the building, but not what comes next:
winning users, as one app among thousands, asking people to leave the tools they know and the data
they've built up there.

Yet most of these ideas don't really compete. A voting tool, a map and a chat could sit side by side
over the same data, each making the others more useful. Instead they're built as islands, fighting
for the same attention, against platforms with billions of users.

## 4. One root cause {#root-cause}

These are one problem seen from two ends, and both come from the sealed box. Social software gets
built as finished, sealed applications, when what people need are experiences they can assemble from
parts.

## 5. Other fields already did this {#other-fields}

Other fields have already made this move.

Games opened up to mods, and some mods outgrew the games they were built on. Music software opened
up to plugins, and a whole industry of instruments and effects grew around one shared standard. Web
publishing opened up to themes and plugins, and WordPress came to run around four in ten websites.

Each time, the recipe was the same. Keep the core small and the conventions clear. Let anyone build
pieces that fit, and let people assemble the pieces they need.

What grows from that isn't a product. It's an ecosystem.

## 6. Why social never did, and what changes {#what-changes}

Social software never made that move, for two reasons. Control of the interface is where the
platforms' business model lives. That's why they never opened it, and why they never can. And
opening a social space is genuinely risky, because code running inside it can read private messages
and act in people's names.

WE works like a games console for social software. The console holds the trusted parts: the building
blocks, the engine that draws them, and the rules for what a cartridge is allowed to use. It doesn't
own your data. It plugs into whatever system a project already uses for that. Each experience is a
cartridge: a forum, a planning board, a whole community app. Swap the cartridge and the same space
becomes something new.

But unlike a real console, no one owns this one. It's open source, and anyone can make a cartridge.
You can open one and read exactly what's inside, because it's data, not code. And you can build new
ones out of pieces of old ones, even cartridges inside cartridges. AI can write one from a sentence,
and any community can pass it on.

## 7. A living system {#living-system}

Picture what that opens up. A community's space grows with it, from ten people to a thousand, and
reshapes itself as the group's purpose shifts. A builder makes the one cartridge they care about,
and it can travel to every community that wants it. Ideas that used to compete start working
together. And every piece anyone adds becomes something everyone else can build on.

That's how open ecosystems have outgrown closed giants before. The open web did, Wikipedia did,
Linux did. Not by building something bigger, but by building something no single company could.

And it changes what software is. It stops being a finished product handed down and starts behaving
like the communities that use it: a living system. Many hands try things, what serves people
spreads, what doesn't fades, and the whole keeps adapting.

Social software has always been something they make for us. Now it can be something we make
together.
