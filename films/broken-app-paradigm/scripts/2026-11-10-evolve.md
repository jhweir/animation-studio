# The broken app paradigm

> Evolvability version (899 words, about 5:35). Written fresh around one idea: social
> software can't evolve, and that's why it keeps failing the groups that use it. Open with a story,
> name the problem, then use variation, selection and inheritance as the spine. A neighbourhood
> mutual-aid group is the thread from start to finish. Possible working title: "Software that can
> evolve".

## 1. A group outgrows its tools {#outgrows}

It starts after a storm. A dozen neighbours make a group chat to check on each other: who has power,
who needs a hand.

A month later there are four hundred people in it. Requests for help scroll past before anyone sees
them. Offers get lost. Someone starts a spreadsheet for the rota, someone else starts a different
one. Decisions happen in whichever thread the right people happened to read.

The group has changed completely. Its tools haven't changed at all.

## 2. Finished software {#finished}

That's not bad luck. It's how social software is made.

A team designs one shape, ships it to millions, and calls it finished. Every group that uses it gets
the same feed, the same threads, the same way of deciding, whatever it's trying to do and however
much it grows.

But communities aren't finished. They grow, split, change purpose. What works for twelve neighbours
breaks at four hundred. The tools stay put while the people inside them move on.

## 3. How anything gets good {#how-things-get-good}

Most of the things that fit us well weren't designed once. They evolved: languages, recipes, cities,
science, open-source software.

Evolution needs three things. Variation: lots of different attempts. Selection: the ones that work
for the people using them survive. Inheritance: what works gets passed on, and built on, instead of
starting again from nothing.

Social software has almost none of the three.

## 4. Why social software is stuck {#stuck}

Variation? A few teams design the tools billions of people use. Very little ever gets tried.

Selection? What survives is what earns its company the most. And a group can't choose the parts that
work for it. It can only pick a whole app, and live with the rest.

Inheritance? Somewhere, someone has already built a brilliant rota tool for a group just like this
one. But it lives inside their app. To reach anyone else, it would have to become a whole new
application, with servers, logins, hosting, and the impossible job of persuading people to move. So
it doesn't reach them, and the next builder starts from scratch.

That's why so many good ideas for social tools never go anywhere. They're built as islands, and
islands can't evolve together.

## 5. Other fields broke out {#broke-out}

Other kinds of software got unstuck. Games opened up to mods, and some mods outgrew the games they
started in. Websites opened up to themes and plugins, and one open system came to run around four in
ten sites on the web.

Each time, the move was the same. Keep the core small. Let anyone build pieces that fit. Let people
choose and combine the pieces they need. Variation, selection and inheritance switched on, and the
whole field took off.

## 6. Why social never did {#why-not}

Social platforms have opened the door a crack, to apps, bots and plug-ins. But always on their
terms, and they've closed it again when it suited them. Control of the interface is where their
business lives.

And there's a real danger. Code running inside a social space can read private messages and act in
people's names. Opening that up carelessly is reckless.

So for social software to evolve, two things have to be true. Opening it has to be safe by design.
And it can't belong to any one company.

## 7. Why now {#why-now}

Something has just changed. AI is making software cheap to build. Soon any group will be able to
describe the tool it needs and get one.

That's a flood of variation. But variation alone isn't evolution. Without a way to choose between
pieces, combine them and pass them on, it's just a million more islands.

What's missing is the ground for all of it to grow in.

## 8. A console no one owns {#console}

That's what WE is. Think of it as a games console for social software.

The console holds the trusted parts: the building blocks, the engine that draws them, and the rules
for what a cartridge is allowed to do. Each cartridge is an experience: a rota, a request board, a
forum, a whole community app. Swap one in, and the same space works in a new way.

You can open any cartridge and see exactly what it does before you let it in. You can build new ones
from pieces of old ones. Your data stays where your community already keeps it. And unlike any real
console, no one owns this one. It's open source, and anyone can build for it.

## 9. Evolution, switched on {#switched-on}

Back to the neighbours.

They find the rota cartridge another group made, and add it to their space. Nobody moves anywhere.
They tweak it to fit the way they work, and their version gets passed to other mutual-aid groups.
One of those groups combines it with a map of who needs what, nearby. Now that's a cartridge too.

Variation: anyone can try something. Selection: the groups living with these tools decide what
spreads. Inheritance: every good idea becomes something the next person can build on.

And the value stops piling up in one company. Every new cartridge makes every space more capable.
Every new community makes every cartridge more worth building.

## 10. Software that grows with us {#grows-with-us}

For twenty years we've tried to design better social software, as if the right team, with the right
intentions, could get the shape right for everyone.

Maybe that was never the answer. The tools we use to live and decide together don't need to be
finished. They need to be able to evolve.

Social software has always been designed for us. Now it can evolve with us.
