# The broken app paradigm

> Full version, v2 (1,264 words, about 7:50). Restructured from 2026-11-03-full: stakes and
> misalignment up front, network effects and funding named, open protocols and past platform openings
> addressed, a "why now" beat, an explicit answer to network effects, and a close that returns to
> collective intelligence. One tenants' association runs through the film as the concrete thread.

## 1. What's at stake {#stakes}

The tools we use to talk to each other do more than carry messages. They shape what we notice, what
we believe, who we find and what we can organise together. They're how billions of people make sense
of the world, collectively. Built well, they could help us become far wiser together than we've ever
been.

Today a handful of companies run them, and they aren't tuned for that. They're tuned to capture
attention and hold it, because attention is what they sell.

## 2. Why alternatives fade {#alternatives}

People keep trying to build something better. Most attempts fade.

Network effects are brutal: people stay where their people are. And money flows to whatever grows
fastest, so tools that won't chase attention rarely get funded.

But there's a third reason, and it's one we can change. Almost every challenger builds the way the
giants do: one team, one sealed application, starting from zero users and competing alone. That's
exactly the game network effects reward, and the giants have already won it.

Open protocols like Mastodon's and Bluesky's have started to free the network underneath. That
matters. But what people actually use on top is still sealed apps, each shaped by one team.

To find different ground, look at what the sealed app costs: the people who use it, and the people
who build it.

## 3. The user's side {#users}

Take a tenants' association: two hundred households in one block. They need to share news, raise
problems, make decisions and hold their landlord to account.

They use a group chat, where important things scroll away. A social media group, where a feed
decides what members see. And a spreadsheet nobody updates.

None of these tools were made for them. Each arrived with one shape, designed once for everyone, and
tuned by someone else for someone else's goals. The group can't change what shows first, how
decisions get made, or how the space works as it grows from twenty active members to two hundred.

There are hundreds of thousands of groups like this, each needing something different, each changing
over time. Even the most caring design team can only serve the common denominator. So communities
conform to whatever fits least badly, or scatter across several tools and lose what came from being
connected.

## 4. The builder's side {#builders}

Now the other side. Somewhere, someone has built exactly what that association needs: a fair way for
a group to raise issues and decide on them together.

To get it to anyone, they've had to wrap it in a whole application: database, servers, logins,
notifications, hosting. Months of foundations that thousands of teams have built before, just to reach
the part that's theirs. Then comes the hardest part: persuading a group to leave the tools they know,
move their people and their history, and bet on a new app that may not last.

So the association never finds it. And a voting tool, a map, a chat and a shared calendar, which could
work side by side over the same data and make each other more useful, get built as islands, competing
for the same attention.

## 5. One root cause {#root-cause}

These are one problem seen from two ends. Social software gets built as finished, sealed
applications, when what communities need are spaces they can assemble from parts, and that anyone
can build parts for.

## 6. Other fields already did this {#other-fields}

Other fields have made this move. Games opened up to mods, and some mods outgrew the games they
started in. Web publishing opened up to themes and plugins, and WordPress came to run around four in
ten websites.

The recipe was the same each time. Keep the core small and the conventions clear. Let anyone build
pieces that fit, and let people assemble what they need. What grows from that isn't a product. It's
an ecosystem.

Social platforms have tried versions of this. Facebook opened up to apps, Discord to bots, Reddit to
third-party clients. But each opening happened on the platform's terms, and when it stopped suiting
them, they shut it. Facebook locked down after Cambridge Analytica. Reddit priced out third-party
apps.

That history shows both obstacles. Control of the interface is where the business model lives, so a
company won't give it up for good. And opening a social space is genuinely risky, because code running
inside it can read private messages and act in people's names. An open layer for social software has
to solve the safety problem, and it can't belong to any one company.

## 7. Why now {#why-now}

Something has shifted. AI is making software cheap to build. Soon a group will be able to describe the
tool it needs and get one.

On its own, that makes the problem worse. A million more sealed apps are a million more islands. When
building is cheap, the bottleneck becomes everything else: trust, distribution, and getting pieces to
work together. That's exactly what a shared, open layer provides.

## 8. A console no one owns {#console}

WE works like a games console for social software. The console holds the trusted parts: the building
blocks, the engine that draws them, and the rules for what a cartridge is allowed to do. Each
experience is a cartridge: a forum, a voting tool, a whole community app. Swap the cartridge and the
same space becomes something new.

But unlike any real console, no one owns this one. It's open source, and anyone can make a cartridge.
Cartridges are made from the console's trusted pieces, not their own code, so you can see exactly
what one does before you let it in. You can build new ones from pieces of old ones, AI can help
draft them, and any community can pass them on.

WE doesn't hold anyone's data hostage either. It plugs into whatever a community already uses,
including open networks like Mastodon and Bluesky. They opened the network. This opens the experience.

Who sets the console's rules, and how the whole thing is sustained, matter as much as the technology.
They belong in the open too, and we'll come back to both.

## 9. Beating network effects {#network-effects}

Here's how this gets around the moat. A builder no longer has to win users from scratch. They make the
one cartridge they care about, and it can travel to every community that wants it, inside the spaces
those communities already have. The tenants' association adds the voting tool without moving
anywhere.

In a sealed world, every network effect builds up for one company. In an open ecosystem, it builds up
for everyone. Every new cartridge makes every space more capable, and every new community makes every
cartridge more worth building. You don't beat a network effect with a bigger app. You beat it with a
bigger, shared one.

That's how the open web, Wikipedia and Linux outgrew closed rivals. Not by being bigger than any one
company, but by becoming something no single company could build.

## 10. Who chooses {#who-chooses}

The deepest change is who decides what spreads.

On today's platforms, design is shaped by what earns ad revenue. In an open ecosystem, it's shaped by
what communities choose to use. That's no guarantee: communities can choose badly too. But the people
choosing are the people affected, and that changes what gets selected for.

Many hands try things. What serves people spreads, what doesn't fades, and the whole keeps adapting.
Software stops being a finished product handed down and starts behaving like the communities that use
it: a living system.

And when the tools that shape how we think together can evolve toward helping us understand each other
and act together, new kinds of collective intelligence become possible.

Social software has always been something they make for us. Now it can be something we make together.
