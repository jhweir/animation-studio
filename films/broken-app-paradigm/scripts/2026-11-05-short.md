# The broken app paradigm

> The short cut (about 3:35), with section 1 as settled on 2026-11-04.

## 1. Fighting on their ground {#their-ground}

A handful of companies now shape how billions of people communicate, find information and
organise. Their reach crept in slowly. Now it's everywhere, and there seems to be no way to push
back.

Alternatives keep appearing, and keep fading. Not because they're worse, but because they all play
by the same rules: one company, one sealed app, handed to millions.

In that game, size and attention win, and the giants already have both.

The paradigm itself is broken, for the people who use these tools and the people who build them.

## 2. The user's side {#users}

Every community needs something different: what to see first, how to decide, how to talk. And
communities are alive. What works for ten people breaks at a thousand.

But the tools arrive with one shape, designed once for everyone. Even the best design team can only
serve the common denominator. So communities either conform to the app that fits least badly, or
fragment across several and lose what connected them.

## 3. The builder's side {#builders}

Builders are stuck too. Say you have an idea for a better social tool: a new way to vote, or a map
of your bioregion. To get it to anyone, you first rebuild a whole app: database, servers, logins,
hosting. Then you fight for users, as one app among thousands.

Yet these ideas don't really compete. A vote, a map and a chat could sit side by side over the same
data. Instead they're islands, fighting for attention against platforms with billions of users.

## 4. One root cause {#root-cause}

One problem, seen from two ends, and both come from the sealed box. People don't need more finished
apps. They need experiences they can assemble from parts.

## 5. Other fields already did this {#other-fields}

Other fields have already made this move. Games opened up to mods, and some mods outgrew the games
themselves. Music software opened up to plugins. Websites opened up to themes. Each time the recipe
was the same: a small core, clear conventions, and pieces anyone can build.

What grows from that isn't a product. It's an ecosystem.

## 6. Why social never did, and what changes {#what-changes}

Social software never made that move, for two reasons. Control of the interface is the platforms'
business model, so they never opened it, and they never can. And opening a social space is risky,
because code inside it can read messages and act in your name.

WE works like a games console for social software. The console holds the trusted parts, and plugs
into whatever system already holds your data. Each experience is a cartridge: a forum, a planning
board, a whole community app. Swap the cartridge and the space becomes something new.

But no one owns this console. It's open source, and anyone can make a cartridge. Each one is data,
not code, so you can read exactly what's inside, and build new ones from old ones. AI can write one
from a sentence, and any community can pass it on.

## 7. A living system {#living-system}

Picture it. A community's space grows with it, from ten people to a thousand. A builder makes one
cartridge, and it can travel to every community that wants it. Ideas that used to compete start
working together.

That's how open ecosystems have outgrown closed giants before: the open web, Wikipedia, Linux. Not by
building something bigger, but by building something no single company could.

Software stops being a product handed down and starts behaving like the communities that use it: a
living system, always adapting.

Social software has always been something they make for us. Now it can be something we make
together.
