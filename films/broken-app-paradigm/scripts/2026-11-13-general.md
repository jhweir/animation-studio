# The broken app paradigm

> General version (877 words, about 5:30). No worked example: the argument is made in
> general terms and carried by the visuals. Users' side: fractal diversity (communities, people,
> one person over time) zoomed through the social-cell holarchy; fixed apps force communities to
> conform or fragment; even a well-meaning central team can't serve the long tail, because the
> knowledge of what's needed sits inside communities. Builders' side: a small idea inside a huge
> rebuilt shell (front end first, backend in one line), then winning users as an island. Root
> cause: the unit is the wrong size. Companion to learning-to-evolve, which picks up the same unit
> over time; evolutionary vocabulary is kept out of this one.

## 1. The shape of our tools {#shape}

The tools we use to talk, organise and decide together shape what our communities can become.

And almost all of them are made the same way. One team designs one application, finishes it, and
hands it to millions.

That shape is failing the people who use these tools, and the people who build them.

## 2. Diversity all the way down {#diversity}

Consider, for a moment, the diversity of our social world.

There are hundreds of thousands of communities online. Choirs and climate groups, research labs and
neighbourhoods, each trying to do something different.

Zoom into any one of them, and every person in it is different too.

Zoom into one person, and even their needs keep changing: from one day to the next, from one role to
another, from one conversation to the next.

And all of it moves. Communities grow, split and change purpose. What works for twelve people breaks
at a thousand.

It's diversity all the way down, and it never stands still.

## 3. Fixed designs {#fixed}

But today's apps are fixed designs: set layouts, a set toolkit, decided in advance by a central team.

They don't adapt to us. So we adapt to them.

Communities either accept an app's limits and bend to fit, or they fragment across several tools and
lose what came from being connected. You've felt it: the decision lost in a chat, the group spread
across five apps.

## 4. An impossible job {#impossible}

It's tempting to blame this on apps built to capture attention and maximise profit. That's part of
it. But it isn't the whole problem.

Imagine a team that truly wanted to serve its users above everything else. It still couldn't.

The people who know what a community needs are inside it, and they can't change their tools. The
people who can change the tools are far away, and they can't see those needs.

So even the best team can only build for the common denominator: the needs most people share. Most
communities live out in the long tail, beyond it. And by the time an app catches up with what a
group needed, the group has moved on.

## 5. The builder's side {#builders}

Now look from the other side, at the people with ideas for better tools.

Most of those ideas are small and specific: a fairer way to make a decision, a map for a bioregion, a
game that helps a group plan. Exactly the kind of thing the long tail needs.

But in this paradigm, an idea can't travel on its own. To reach anyone, it has to become a whole
application. So the builder rebuilds the entire social shell around it: sign-up and profiles, feeds
and threads, messages and notifications, roles, moderation, onboarding, a design system, web and
mobile apps. And beneath all that, servers, databases and hosting.

The idea is a small fraction of the work. The shell is everything else.

## 6. Islands {#islands}

AI is making that shell faster to build. But the hardest part comes after.

The finished app is an island. To be used, it has to win people from scratch: persuade a community
to leave the tools it knows, move its people and its history, and bet on something new.

So most good ideas never reach the people they were made for. And tools that could work together,
over the same people and the same data, end up competing for the same attention.

## 7. The wrong size {#wrong-size}

These are one problem seen from two ends.

Communities' needs are fine-grained and always changing. Builders' ideas are small and specific. But
the only unit this paradigm offers is the whole application: too big to fit anyone's needs exactly,
and too big for most good ideas to ever ship.

The unit is the wrong size.

## 8. Other software got smaller pieces {#other-fields}

Other kinds of software solved this. Games opened up to mods. Music software opened up to plugins.
Websites opened up to themes and plugins, and one open system came to run around four in ten sites
on the web.

Each time, the move was the same: a small shared core, and pieces anyone can build that fit
together.

## 9. Why social never did {#why-not}

Social platforms have opened the door a crack, to apps and bots, but on their own terms, and they've
closed it again when it suited them. Control of the interface is where their business lives.

And opening a social space is genuinely risky. Code running inside it can read private messages and
act in people's names. So an open layer for social software has to be safe by design, and it can't
belong to any one company.

## 10. Pieces the right size {#right-size}

That's what WE is built to be: an open core for social software, owned by no one.

The core holds the trusted parts: the building blocks, the engine that draws them, and the rules for
what each piece may do. On top of it, anyone can build pieces: a way to decide, a map, a forum, a
whole community space. You can see exactly what a piece does before you let it in.

Now a builder makes just the part that's theirs, and it can reach every community that wants it,
inside the spaces they already have. And a community can assemble what fits, and reshape it as it
grows and changes.

Social software has always been something they make for us. Now it can be something we make
together.
